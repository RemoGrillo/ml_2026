FP16
Floating Point, 16 bits. Like scientific notation: sign, exponent (how big), mantissa (the digits). Models are trained and released like this.
BF16
Brain Float 16 (Google Brain): same 16 bits, more exponent, fewer digits. Wide range, less detail.
FP32
Full precision, 32 bits: what training uses. Twice FP16.
Q8 · Q6 · Q4
Quantized to 8, 6, 4 bits: each weight becomes a small integer; one scale per block of ~32 weights restores the size.
Q4_K_M
GGUF naming. 4 bits · K = k-quant, blocks inside super-blocks · S / M / L = small, medium, large: how many sensitive layers keep extra bits.
GGUF · MLX
File formats: GGUF for llama.cpp, Ollama, LM Studio; MLX for Apple Silicon.
the trade

Q8 ≈ lossless, Q6–Q5 safe, Q4 the usual sweet spot; below, quality drops.

size of an 8B model

16 GB at FP16 · 8.5 at Q8 · 4.8 at Q4

Precision here = numerical precision of each weight, not precision-and-recall. Grey: original weights. Red: what survives with 2bits levels. Real formats (GGUF Q4_K, MLX 4-bit) quantize small blocks, each with its own scale, so the error is smaller than shown.
Quantization stores each weight with fewer bits. First the acronyms, on the right. FP16 is a 16-bit floating-point number, like scientific notation in binary: one sign bit, five exponent bits that say how big the number is, ten mantissa bits for the digits (the strips under the chart show the layout). BF16, "brain float" from Google Brain, keeps the same 16 bits but moves them: eight for the exponent, seven for the digits, so it covers a much wider range with less detail; it is common in training. FP32 is full precision. The Q formats are quantized: each weight becomes a small integer, and one scale factor per block of about 32 weights brings it back to the right magnitude. In GGUF names like Q4_K_M, K means k-quants (blocks grouped in super-blocks, each with its own scale) and S, M, L say how many of the most sensitive layers are kept at higher precision. The strips are clickable too. The word has nothing to do with precision and recall: it's numerical precision. Click the buttons: with 8 bits you have 256 levels and the red line is indistinguishable from the grey; at 4 bits, 16 levels, you see steps; at 2 bits only 4 levels. Real formats are smarter than this toy: they quantize blocks of 32 or 64 weights, each block with its own scale, so the error is much smaller. Practical rule: Q8 is essentially lossless, Q6 and Q5 safe, Q4 the usual compromise, below Q4 quality degrades noticeably. And the size halves at each step.

Will it fit? Weights + KV cache + system.

rule of thumb

4-bit weights ≈ parameters (in billions) × 0.6 GB.

plus

The KV cache for your context, plus the ~8 GB your system already uses.

where it lives

Apple Silicon: one shared pool. Discrete GPU: past VRAM, layers spill to CPU and slow sharply.

KV estimate assumes a typical grouped-query-attention model with FP16 cache; real architectures vary by ×0.5–×2.
A calculator you can use before downloading anything. Weights: parameters times bytes per weight (about 0.6 GB per billion at 4-bit, including overhead). KV cache: proportional to context length. System: roughly 8 GB already used by the OS and your apps. Try 8B at Q4 with 8k on 16 GB: fits. Push context to 128k: it doesn't. Try 27B at Q8 on 32 GB: too large, which is exactly LM Studio's verdict on the next slide. On a Mac, CPU and GPU share memory; on a PC with a discrete GPU, what doesn't fit in VRAM is offloaded to the CPU and runs several times slower.

under the hood · 11mixture of experts

Mixture of experts: everything stored, little used.

 
qwen3-35b-a3b
35b

total parameters, all experts → decides memory: ≈ 20 GB at Q4.

a3b

active per token → decides speed: about as fast as a 3B.

the picture

A hospital with 128 specialists: each patient sees 8, but all 128 must be on the payroll.

not topic experts

No “Latin expert”: the router learns its own split, token by token, layer by layer.

other spellings

8x7B: 8 experts, 2 per token (Mixtral: ~47B stored, ~13B active).

A dense model uses every parameter for every token. A mixture-of-experts model replaces the big feed-forward block of each layer with many smaller "experts" and a router. Watch the animation: each word of the sentence arrives at the router, which sends it to two of the eight experts; only those two compute, then their outputs are combined and passed to the next layer. The small counters show that over a sentence the work spreads across all experts, so all of them must be loaded in memory, even though each token only uses a quarter of them. Real models have 64 to 128 experts with about 8 active. That is what the name encodes. In qwen3-35b-a3b, "35b" is the total: all experts, which is what has to fit in memory, about 20 GB at 4 bits. "a3b" means 3 billion active per token, which is what sets the speed: it writes about as fast as a 3B dense model. The analogy: a hospital with 128 specialists, every patient sees 8, but you pay all 128. Careful: experts are not topic specialists; the split is learned and not human-readable. Older naming: Mixtral 8x7B, eight experts of roughly 7B, two active, about 47B stored and 13B active because attention layers are shared.

Why this matters in practice. Three models that all "feel" like a 3–4B when they write: similar speed, because about the same number of parameters is active per token. But look at the files. Qwen3-4B is dense: 4 billion stored, 4 billion used, 2.5 GB at 4 bits. Gemma's E4B means 4B effective: about 4 billion used per token, but the file is almost three times larger, because extra parameters (per-layer embeddings) are stored and looked up rather than computed with. The MoE model qwen3-35b-a3b uses 3 billion per token but needs about 20 GB of memory. So: the active number tells you how fast it will write; the file size tells you whether it fits on your laptop at all.

Four limits of a model on its own, each with a small example. Cutoff: ask about something after its training date and it either says so or, worse, guesses. Your documents: it has never seen your letters, your draft, your catalogue. No sources: it may tell you Datini died in 1410, and it may be right, but it can't point to where that comes from. Gaps: ask what Datini wrote to Stoldo on a precise day and it will produce a fluent, entirely invented letter. And pasting the archive into the prompt is not a way out: ten thousand OCR'd pages are about six million tokens, against a context window of about a hundred thousand. Look at the two bars.
RAG · 02three options

Three ways to give a model your documents.

paste it allfine-tuneretrieve, then generate

Three strategies. Paste everything: works for one long document, fails for a collection, and every question re-reads all of it. Fine-tuning: the knowledge gets baked into weights, updates need retraining, there's no citation, and in practice fine-tuning shapes style and format better than it injects reliable facts. RAG: search the collection first and give the model only the few passages that matter. Cheap per question, easy to update, citable. Its weak point, which we'll keep seeing, is retrieval quality.
RAG · 03Lewis et al., 2020
Rretrieval

Search your collection for the passages closest to the question.

Aaugmented

Put those passages into the prompt, next to the question.

Ggeneration

The LLM answers from them, and can cite them.

An open-book exam: the model doesn't memorise the library, it looks things up before answering.
The three letters. Retrieval: a search step over your own documents. Augmented: the retrieved passages are added to the prompt. Generation: the language model writes the answer conditioned on those passages. The term comes from Lewis et al., 2020. The best analogy is an open-book exam: we don't ask the model to remember, we give it the right pages.
RAG · 04two phases

Two phases: index once, ask many times.

 
indexing

Slow, done once; redo only when documents change.

querying

Fast, per question: one embedding, one search, one LLM call.

Every RAG system has these two phases, and the animation follows one object through each. Indexing, top row, offline: a page becomes text, the text is cut into chunks, each chunk becomes a vector, and the vectors go into the store together with their text and source. Two documents go through; the grey cells were indexed earlier. Querying, bottom row, at every question: the question is embedded with the same model, the search compares it with every vector in the store and lights the three nearest, those chunks and the question are assembled into a prompt, the LLM answers, and the answer carries the sources of the chunks it used. RAG · 05chunking

Chunking: cut documents into retrievable pieces.

 
current chunkoverlap with the previous chunk
why

Embeddings work best on paragraph-sized passages; the prompt has room for only a few.

chunk size

Too small: loses context. Too large: dilutes relevance, costs tokens.

overlap

Neighbours share a margin, so a sentence cut at a border survives whole.

strategies

Fixed characters or tokens · sentences, paragraphs · pages, headings.

Demo: 220 characters, overlap 50 · notebook: 1000 / 200 characters · rag_app: 1024 / 32 tokens.
Chunking decides what a "passage" is. The animation slides a fixed window over a text: each chunk is 220 characters, and each one starts 50 characters before the previous one ended; the solid orange is that overlap. Without overlap, a key sentence cut exactly at a border would be split across two chunks and match neither well. Size is a trade-off: small chunks are precise but lose context; large chunks carry context but dilute the match and consume prompt tokens. Beyond fixed windows you can split on sentences, paragraphs or document structure, and for OCR'd material keeping the page number on every chunk is what makes citations possible.
RAG · 06embeddings

Embeddings: meaning as coordinates.

text → vector

A model maps a passage to a list of numbers. nomic-embed-text: 768.

near = similar

Distance tracks meaning, not shared words.

one model for both

Chunks and questions must be embedded by the same model.

[0.021, -0.143, 0.088, 0.310, … ] # 768 numbers
2-D sketch of a 768-dimensional space; positions and scores are illustrative.
An embedding model turns any text into a fixed-length vector, 768 numbers for nomic-embed-text, such that texts with similar meaning end up close together. It's the same idea as the word embeddings from earlier in the course, applied to whole passages. Watch: our chunks form neighbourhoods (art history, poetry, music, and some unrelated text); then a question arrives, "Why was Caravaggio always in trouble with the police?", and lands next to the passages on his brawls, police records and death sentence, even though it shares almost no exact words with them. The three closest chunks are what retrieval returns. One rule: chunks and questions must be embedded with the same model, or the coordinates mean nothing.

similarity · 01word arithmetic

Words are numbers: you can add and subtract them.

king − man + woman ≈ queen
Paris − France + Italy ≈ Rome
walking − walk + swim ≈ swimming
better − good + bad ≈ worse
every word is a vector

king = [0.50, −0.12, 0.83, …]: a few hundred numbers, learned from text.

directions mean something

man → king and woman → queen are the same arrow: “royalty”.

Classic results with word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013): the computed point's nearest word. 2-D sketch of a 300-dimensional space.
This is the "aha". In an embedding space every word is a vector, a list of a few hundred numbers learned from how words are used. And the geometry carries meaning: the arrow from man to king is roughly the same arrow as from woman to queen. So you can compute: start at king, subtract man, add woman, and the nearest word to where you land is queen. The animation cycles through four classic examples from word2vec: royalty, capitals, verb forms, comparatives. These are nearest-neighbour results, not exact equalities, hence the ≈. The embedding models we use for RAG do the same thing for whole sentences and paragraphs.
similarity · 02cosine, a refresher

Cosine: the shadow of one arrow on another.

θ = 0°cos θ = 1.00
how to read it

Arrow b has length 1. Drop a line from its tip onto a: the length of that shadow is cos θ.

0°1

same direction

90°0

nothing in common

180°−1

opposite

from school

In a right triangle: cos θ = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse.

A two-minute refresher, because "cosine similarity" contains two words and the first one deserves a picture. Arrow a is fixed. Arrow b, of length 1, rotates. Drop a perpendicular from the tip of b onto a: the orange segment, the shadow of b on a, is the cosine of the angle between them. Same direction: the shadow is the whole arrow, cosine 1. At 90 degrees the shadow disappears: cosine 0. Pointing the opposite way: the shadow is on the other side, cosine −1. It is the school formula, adjacent over hypotenuse, with the hypotenuse set to 1. So cosine measures how much one arrow points along the other, regardless of how long they are.
similarity · 03cosine similarity

Cosine similarity: do two texts point the same way?

both words, together

Similarity: texts become vectors, close meaning = close direction. Cosine: measures that direction, ignoring length.

cos(q, a) = q · a / (‖q‖ ‖a‖)
# by hand, in 2-D q = (3, 4) a = (4, 3) q · a = 3·4 + 4·3 = 24 ‖q‖ = ‖a‖ = 5 cos = 24 / (5 · 5) = 0.96 # almost the same direction
# all chunks at once, in numpy (768-D) scores = (M / norm(M, axis=1, keepdims=True)) @ (q / norm(q))
Now both words. Similarity: the embedding model turns a question and a chunk into vectors, and texts with close meaning point in close directions. Cosine: the measure from the previous slide, the shadow of one arrow on the other, which ignores how long the arrows are, so a long chunk and a short question can still match. The formula: dot product divided by the two lengths. By hand in two dimensions: q = (3, 4), a = (4, 3), dot product 24, both lengths 5, cosine 0.96. With 768 dimensions it is the same arithmetic, and numpy does it for every chunk at once in one line: normalise, then one matrix product. That line is the notebook's retriever.

temperature 0 · why not identical?

T = 0 picks the top token. The top token itself can change.

1 · floating point

On a GPU, (a + b) + c ≠ a + (b + c) in the last digits. Sums are split across thousands of threads, and the order is not always the same.

2 · batching

Your request is processed together with other users' requests. A different batch → different kernels and summation order → slightly different logits.

3 · near-ties

Usually harmless. But when two tokens are almost tied (say 0.4001 vs 0.3999), a tiny change flips the winner…

4 · autoregression

…and the flipped token becomes context for every later step: from there on, the two texts diverge.

5 · the model itself

Providers update models behind the same name; some mixture-of-experts routing depends on the batch too.

locally

Same weights, same software, same machine, one request at a time: usually identical, run after run.
